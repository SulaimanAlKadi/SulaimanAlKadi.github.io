Starling

Correct transient-absorption data and fit its kinetics. Drop in the files your spectrometer wrote and it finds time zero at every probe wavelength, removes the coherent artifact and scattered pump, and tells you how many lifetimes the data actually support. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.

1 Load your data

Drop one file, several, or a whole folder. Comma or tab separated text from any instrument, Ultrafast Systems .ufs, or .npz. Delays may run down the first column or across the top row; it works out which from the numbers.

2 Choose what to do

The defaults are the right answer for most data. Open the panels only if you need to change something.

Chirp and instrument response

The physical model constrains time zero to a real group delay through glass, so it cannot wander where the data are weak. Joint refinement fits the chirp, the instrument response and the lifetimes together, which is what keeps chirp uncertainty inside the lifetime error bars instead of quietly disappearing from them.

Scattered pump

Scattered pump is flat in delay and far narrower than any electronic transition, so it can be modeled and subtracted rather than masked out, which gives you back the wavelengths nearest the excitation. If the feature turns out to be too broad to be a laser line, nothing is removed and it says so. Files from Ultrafast Systems usually carry the pump wavelength and it is read automatically.

Lifetimes

Choosing the number of exponentials is not really a statistics question, so Starling does not pretend it is. It refits at each count and keeps the largest one whose lifetimes do not move when another component is added. A lifetime shorter than the instrument response is marked and never counted, however well it fits.

Several datasets together

A chirp belongs to the optics, not the sample, so a set of measurements from one sitting should share one curve; fitting each file separately lets the correction drift between samples you mean to compare. For a fluence or titration series, sharing the lifetimes uses the fact that only the amplitudes should change; the spectra stay free for every dataset.

Vibrational coherence

After the lifetimes are taken out, what is left oscillating is a wavepacket. The amplitude and phase across the probe are what separate a ground-state wavepacket from an excited-state one; a frequency that matches the solvent and shows no structure across the probe is the solvent. The window must sit inside the evenly sampled part of the delay axis, and it will be clipped if it does not.

Posterior sampling

Variable projection gives one optimum and a quadratic approximation around it. Sampling instead gives the shape: intervals that can be asymmetric, the correlation that shows when two rates are really one parameter, and a credible band on each species spectrum. Parallel and sequential schemes with the same rates fit identically, so the choice changes what the spectra mean, not how well it fits.

3 Fit exponentials

Fits a set of lifetimes shared across the whole probe, and will work out how many the data support rather than being told. This runs on its own, so the number of components can be changed without redoing the chirp, which is the slow part.

How many, and what counts as resolvable

The instrument response decides this answer as much as the data do: a lifetime shorter than twice its sigma is the response itself, and is never counted however well it fits. It is a property of your instrument rather than something the map can be asked, because a wider response and a faster decay describe the same rise. Leave it blank and it comes from the chirp fit, or from a stated default on a map that arrived already corrected. If the answer below says nothing is supportable, this is usually why.

Almost every map has something outliving the window. A model without a species for it stretches its longest lifetime to cover it and reports that with a tight error bar.

What to fit it to

On work out what this map needs each option is tried on a thinned copy and kept or discarded by how flat time zero ends up, which is the job the correction exists to do. A map that arrives corrected is left alone: straightening it again would only smear it.

Worth narrowing when part of the probe is dead, or when scattered pump sits in the window.

4 Results

What it does

In ultrafast experiments, white-light probes are chirped by the material they pass through, such as the quartz windows of the sample cuvette. This gives every wavelength its own time zero. Most transient-absorption analysis programs leave chirp correction to the user, who drags a crude set of points through the surface by eye. This takes valuable time away from researchers, and it also leads to meaningful differences in the extracted data, particularly in the kinetics. Starling fits time zero at every wavelength at once, constrained to a physical group delay, and then removes the coherent artifact on the raw grid before any resampling. Resampling first would smear the artifact across delay bins and leave a residue that nothing can remove afterward. The chirp, the instrument response, and the lifetimes are refined together, so the uncertainty in time zero stays inside the error bar on your fastest lifetime instead of vanishing from it. Importantly, poor fits are flagged: across a validation set of 40 simulated experiments, every run whose time-zero error exceeded 25% of the instrument-response width raised a warning, so the user knows to inspect it more closely.
