SuperSpinner

Extracting spin Hamiltonian parameters for an S = ½ system from a CW-EPR spectrum is often a labor- and computationally intensive task. SuperSpinner extracts the g and hyperfine tensors, with uncertainties, directly from a CW-EPR spectrum in less than a second. The program also identifies the central nucleus, the species family, linewidths and strains, and secondary species in a single pass. The network runs locally in your browser, and no data is uploaded or saved anywhere. Give the demo a try!

1 See it work

When training a neural network, one splits the dataset into a training set and a test set; because the model never sees the test set during training, the test set is what is used to assess its accuracy. Crucially, because this model is trained (mostly) on ideal synthetic spectra generated with EasySpin, it would not, on its own, learn to handle the problems that come with real measurements. The solution is to degrade the synthetic data deliberately. Here, this is done by choosing a random sweep window and point density and by adding noise (down to a signal-to-noise ratio of 2), drift, linear and nonlinear baselines, spikes, and broad background features. All of these are added on top of each simulated spectrum, which makes the training more robust. Mess around with the model yourself below!

3 How it works

    
A one-dimensional ResNet with dilated convolutions feeds two Transformer layers and a set of multi-task heads. The g turning points and the outer hyperfine lines are localized by keypoint heatmaps decoded with a soft-argmax, which gives sub-pixel field positions where direct regression plateaus; direct regression heads supply the remaining parameters and serve as the fallback when a line falls outside the measured window. The training set is 60,000 EasySpin simulations of S = ½ centers drawn from eight chemically informed prior families, mixed on the fly with secondary species and measurement artifacts. On 3,000 held-out spectra the median error in g is 0.003 and the central nucleus is identified blind three times out of four. The prediction is also a good starting point for a conventional EasySpin refinement.
